The brush paints the world; the villagers make it a kingdom. Brushland's second half — the simulation that answers your painting — is what separates it from every pure terrain-painting toy, and this page covers it as demonstrated: how settlers arrive, how they choose where to live, how houses adapt to surroundings, and what your painting decisions actually mean for the people who move in. It pairs with the paint mechanics guide; together they are the whole game.

## Settlers Who Choose for Themselves

The foundational behavior, confirmed by the reveal: villagers arrive in your painted world unprompted and settle it on their own judgment. You never place a home, zone a district, or assign a settler — the game's inhabitants read the world your strokes made and decide where to live by their own lights. The trailers show settlers walking into frame and beginning cottages in spots that make sense: near water, on gentle ground, in the shelter of your painted geography. The design consequence is subtle and enormous — Brushland inverts the power fantasy of the builder genre. You are not the kingdom's architect; you are its landscape, and the kingdom organizes itself around you. Every painting decision becomes an act of urban planning made indirectly: paint a river and you have designed where the village will grow, without ever drawing its streets.

## Houses That Change With Their Surroundings

The claim the trailers go furthest to prove: houses in Brushland change appearance depending on what surrounds them. The reveal cuts the same architecture against different settings — and the cottages visibly dress differently by context, adapting to the geography your brush created. The mechanism as shown implies real contextual simulation: the houses are reading their environment (elevation, water, neighbors, terrain type) and expressing it in their look, which means your painting vocabulary is also an architectural vocabulary. Paint alpine terrain and your settlers' homes answer it; paint riverside meadows and the architecture answers that instead. For a painting game, this is the loop that gives strokes permanence and meaning: every landform you grow eventually shows up in the rooftops of the people who came for it.

## What Your Brushing Means for Their Lives

The interpretive layer, connecting the two halves: in Brushland, your aesthetic decisions are the kingdom's circumstances. Where you paint water determines who lives near it; where you raise mountains determines what shelters what; your roads suggest how the settlements connect. The trailers' most emotional beat depends on this loop — watching a village grow around a landscape you personally stroked into existence, recognizing your brushwork in the geography of their lives. The simulation's depth beyond the demonstrated (needs, roles, families, events) is unconfirmed and honestly labeled so; what is confirmed is the relationship itself, and it is the game's soul.

## The Cozy Simulation Difference

Why this design lands differently from a management sim: Brushland's villagers are presented as inhabitants of a picture book, not line items in a ledger. No needs meters over their heads, no complaint queues, no optimization pressure — the settling simulation exists to make your painting feel loved, not to hand you problems. That choice defines the audience (cozy sandbox people, not spreadsheet people) and the game's likely ceiling as a relaxation experience: the kingdom is your painting's reward, delivered as neighbors who moved in because they liked what you made.

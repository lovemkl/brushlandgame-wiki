In Brushland, the brush is the game. Everything the player does passes through it: mountains are strokes, rivers are strokes, roads are strokes — and the kingdom grows out of whatever you paint. This guide covers the paint mechanics as demonstrated in the reveal materials: each brush the trailers show, how the geography responds, what editing and revision appear to look like, and the open questions the community is already asking. It is written from the October 2026 reveal state and updated as the studio shows more — confirmed behavior and reasonable inference are labeled apart.

## The Mountain Brush: Stroking Landforms

The reveal trailers show terrain painting most directly in their mountain sequences: a stroke lays down a ridge line, and the land swells along it into proper mountains — not voxel towers, not heightmap sliders, but rock that reads as painted geology, complete with the fairytale proportions Brushland's art style demands. The visible behavior suggests stroke-then-growth: you draw the intention (a ridge here, running this way), and the system grows the landform with its own internal logic — slopes, shoulders, the way a real mountain range has personality beyond the line you drew. That gap between your stroke and the grown result is where Brushland's magic lives: you are not sculpting every contour, you are commissioning geology.

What the materials imply about scale: strokes appear to work at multiple granularities — broad sweeps for major ranges, smaller touches for hills and adjustments — and the painting sequences show landforms landing in real time without loading or generation pauses, which for a terrain-growing system is itself an engineering flex. The community's open questions — brush sizes, tapering, whether strokes can layer to build higher peaks — are exactly the details a hands-on preview will answer first, and this page updates when it does.

## The Water Brushes: Rivers, Waterfalls, Lakes

Water is where Brushland's terrain-as-paint concept shows its depth, because water in a painted world has to behave, not just render. The trailers demonstrate the centerpiece: draw a valley and rivers fill it, following your painted elevation — and when your land drops sharply, the river pours off the edge as a waterfall, unprompted and glorious. That single behavior implies the whole system: water reads the geography you painted, finds its paths, and expresses them — meaning your mountain strokes and valley strokes are also hydrology decisions, made with the same brush. Paint a plateau edge and you have commissioned a waterfall; paint a basin and you have asked for a lake.

For a kingdom-builder, this matters beyond beauty. The villagers settle the world your water shapes — homes near rivers, lives organized around the features your strokes created — which makes the water brushes the closest thing Brushland has to a planning tool: paint the watershed you want your kingdom to live around, and the kingdom's geography writes itself. The painting system page covers the full terrain-water interaction model as demonstrated; this page's summary is the operative truth: in Brushland, you do not place water — you paint land, and water answers.

## The Road Brush: Kingdom Connective Tissue

The third demonstrated brush is the road stroke — paths that wind into painted terrain, connecting the places your villagers settle. The trailers show roads appearing along drawn routes, adapting to the geography they cross — bending around your mountains, following your valleys, behaving like paths that surveyors walked rather than lines that were stamped. Roads in Brushland appear to be the player's way of suggesting circulation to the kingdom: the villagers choose where to live, and your roads shape how their world connects — the softest possible version of infrastructure, expressed as another stroke of the brush.

What roads imply about the simulation: a game only draws adaptive roads if its terrain model is genuinely continuous — no grid underneath, or a fine enough one to be invisible. Every wound-in road in the reveal is evidence that the painted world is a real world, geometrically speaking, which is exactly the engineering foundation the villager AI needs to do its settling magic. The roads are also the mechanic most likely to surprise at release with depth: path networks shape trade, visits, and the life of the kingdom in ways cozy reveals rarely catalog, and this page tracks what the studio shows about it. And a quiet design observation worth keeping: of the three brushes, roads are the most social — mountains and water paint the world for its own sake, but a road is painted for the villagers, a stroke whose entire meaning is someone else's journey. Brushland's brush set, read closely, already tells you what kind of game it wants to be: a painting game that keeps noticing its people.

## Editing, Undo, and Repainting

The coziest question about a painting game: what happens when you change your mind? The reveal materials gesture at revision — sequences show terrain being adjusted and landforms evolving as painting continues — but the full editing model (erase strokes, overpaint landforms, lower mountains, reroute rivers) is not yet demonstrated end-to-end. This is the guide's most update-prone section, and the honest state is: Brushland's entire loop depends on forgiving revision. A painting game that punishes early strokes dies in its first hour; a painting game that lets you repaint your mistakes lives forever. Everything about Transhuman Design's systems pedigree suggests they know this, and the trailer's adjustment sequences suggest it is built. The details — and they will matter — come with the next showings.
